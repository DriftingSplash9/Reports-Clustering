 2.1.1 Concepts and definitions Analytical Framework: The underlying framework for the analytical accounts of the central bank is a statement that is intended to summarize conceptually the factors that affect reserve balances of depository institutions. In the U.S., the monetary authority is defined as the Federal Reserve, together with certain monetary accounts of the Treasury. Thus, the statement of factors affecting reserve balances is a consolidation of the accounts on the Reserve Bank balance sheets and the Treasury's monetary accounts. The H.4.1 release, "Factors Affecting Reserve Balances of Depository Institutions and Condition Statement of Federal Reserve Banks," includes the statement of factors affecting reserve balances of depository institutions, as well as the balance sheets of the Federal Reserve Banks. The H.3 release, "Aggregate Reserves of Depository Institutions and the Monetary Base," formerly included data on aggregate reserves of depository institutions and the monetary base. It ceased publication after September 24, 2020 and was consolidated in the H.6 release, "Money Stock Measures." Changes in the stance of monetary policy are implemented primarily through open market operations that affect the composition of reserves and, in the longer run, the overall quantity of reserves. Data on the types and quantities of open market operations are used by the public to gain insights into the implementation of monetary policy. Through these transactions, the Federal Reserve attempts to influence prices of financial assets and the pace of expansion of money and credit in order to achieve the nation's objectives for macroeconomic variables such as inflation, economic growth, and the balance of international payments. The concepts and definitions are consistent with Monetary and Financial Statistics Manual and Compilation Guide (Washington, D.C: IMF, 2016) with the following exceptions: Beginning in October 2008, data refer to the last Wednesday of the month rather than month end. Financial derivatives are excluded from the data. Prior to December 2007, central bank liquidity swaps are excluded. By law, the Federal Reserve System may not retain earnings, but the Reserve Banks may retain an aggregate amount in "surplus", which is set at a statutory limit. Surplus funds that exceed the statutory limit must be remitted to the Treasury. If earnings become less than the cost of operations, payment of dividends, and reservation of an amount necessary to maintain surplus, Reserve Banks suspend weekly remittances and record a deferred asset. Holdings of US Government and Federal agency securities are valued at acquisition cost rather than current market price or fair value. 2.3.1 Classification/sectorization Classification: The statement of factors affecting reserve balances is divided into factors supplying reserve balances to depository institutions and factors absorbing reserve balances. Total reserve balances available to depository institutions to meet their reserve requirements are equal to total factors supplying reserve balances less total factors absorbing reserve balances. The statement of factors is closely related to the Reserve Bank balance sheet identity, through which total balances held by depositories may be calculated as total Federal Reserve assets less all Federal Reserve liabilities excluding these balances. Therefore, factors supplying reserve balances correspond for the most part to asset categories on the Federal Reserve balance sheet, while factors absorbing reserve balances correspond to liability categories. There are exceptions to the above rule that relate to the netting of some Federal Reserve liabilities against assets, or that involve the inclusion of the Treasury's monetary accounts in the definitions of some entries. The exceptions are: (1) Float: cash items in the process of collection (an asset item on the Federal Reserve's balance sheet) less deferred availability cash items (a liability item) plus float-related as-of adjustments (as-of adjustments are balance sheet memorandum items used by Reserve Banks to adjust the reserve positions of depository institutions, mainly to offset the cumulative impact of errors affecting a depository institution's account balance at its Reserve Bank); (2) Gold stock: gold certificates (an asset item) plus nonmonetized gold stock (a Treasury account); (3) Treasury currency outstanding: coin (an asset item) plus Treasury currency held by the Treasury and by the public (both of which are Treasury items); (4) Currency in circulation: Federal Reserve notes (a liability item) plus Treasury currency held by the public (a Treasury item); (5) Treasury cash holdings: nonmonetized gold stock plus Treasury currency held by the Treasury and Federal Reserve notes held by the Treasury (Treasury items); (6) Other assets: other assets (an asset item) plus non-float related as-of adjustments (an off-balance sheet item); and, (7) Service-related balances and adjustments: required clearing balances and adjustments to compensate for float. Movements in Treasury items have no effect on total reserve balances because any such movements are offset in the construction of currency in circulation, Treasury outstanding, Treasury cash holdings, and gold stock. Scope of the data Institutional Coverage: The monetary authority in the U.S. is defined as the Federal Reserve, together with certain monetary accounts of the Treasury. No units of the central bank are excluded from these accounts. Depository institutions operating in the U.S. are required by law to maintain reserves against certain deposit liabilities. These reserves may take the form of vault cash or reserve balances held at Federal Reserve Banks. The amount of reserve balances available to the banking system is affected by the monetary activities of the public, the Treasury, depository institutions, and the Federal Reserve. 2.4.1 Valuation Accounting Practices: The U.S. government securities and Federal agency obligations are valued at face value. The triparty repurchase agreements are valued at cash value. Positions with the IMF, included in the special drawing rights (SDRs) certificate credits that are held by the Reserve Banks, are valued monthly, based on the exchange rates of a number of the underlying currencies. The Treasury's specific monetary account items are valued at the book value of the monetary gold stock, silver dollars and fractional coin outstanding, various types of paper currency outstanding (excluding Federal Reserve notes), and Treasury cash holdings. Assets denominated in foreign currencies (which are included in "other F.R. assets") include those acquired in intervention or through "swap" drawings, and include funds held for the Treasury and the Exchange Stabilization Fund (ESF). Foreign currency, if bought in the exchange markets, initially is valued at the average cost. Foreign currencies are revalued to market price two business days prior to the end of each month; the revaluation is recorded on the last business day of the month. 3.1.1 Source data collection programs Data sources: The data are constructed primarily from the consolidated balance sheet of the Federal Reserve System. Also included are certain off-balance-sheet items of the Federal Reserve Banks and selected data from the Treasury's monetary accounts. Each Reserve Bank reports its balance sheet data as of the close of business on the FR 34 report. This report, after passing validity and quality checks, is transmitted electronically to the Board of Governors on the afternoon of the next business day. The off-balance-sheet items (float-related as-ofs, non-float-related as-ofs, and adjustments to compensate for float) are transmitted via a separate daily wire from each Reserve Bank to the Board of Governors, where they are edited for accuracy and then aggregated. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York sends data on the securities-related items to the Board by fax each morning. Information regarding the Treasury's monetary accounts is communicated to Board staff by letter or by telephone with lags as long as a month or more. The specific monetary account items are the book value of the monetary gold stock, silver dollars and fractional coin outstanding, various types of paper currency outstanding (excluding Federal Reserve notes), and Treasury cash holdings. The data on the reserves of depository institutions are constructed from weekly and quarterly reports that are submitted to the Federal Reserve by depository institutions that are required to hold reserves. Data for depository institutions that are not required to hold reserves are needed to construct the currency component of the money stock, which is a component of the monetary base; for these institutions, data are provided in the quarterly Call Report submitted to their primary regulator. 3.3.2 Other statistical procedures Statistical adjustments: The data on aggregate reserves of depository institutions and the monetary base are published on both a seasonally adjusted and not seasonally adjusted basis. In addition, the data on reserves are published as both "adjusted" and "not adjusted" for regulatory changes in reserve requirements. (Changes in reserve requirements create discontinuities or "breaks" in the data. To adjust for these changes, a multiplicative procedure is used to estimate what required reserves would have been in past period had current reserve requirements be in effect.) 3.4.1 Validation of intermediate results Data verification: Before transmitting any data to the Board, each Reserve Bank checks its own balance sheet data, as well as the data supplied to it by the depository institutions within its district. Staff at the Board perform additional validity and quality checks on the data. The off-balance-sheet items are also checked at the Board. Staff at the Board and at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York check each other's calculations to minimize the chance of arithmetic error. Data are released simultaneously by the Board's Public Affairs Office and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's Public Information Office. 3.4.2 Assessment of intermediate data Owing to the reporting lags, Treasury items for a given date typically are estimated for several weeks until Board staff receive the actual data. Such estimates are constructed by carrying forward the last reported value. This method is reasonably accurate because the Treasury's monetary accounts are not particularly volatile from week to week. Moreover, there is no practical concern with errors in estimating these components over a short time because movements in the Treasury's accounts do not affect total reserve balances. 3.5.1 Revision studies and analyses The statement of factors affecting reserve balances of depository institutions and the Federal Reserve balance sheet data are typically subject to very little revision. Revisions can stem from two sources: changes in the underlying data, and errors. Changes in the underlying data can occur because of the application of as-of adjustments to prior weeks, or the substitution of actual data for previously estimated data. Data on the Treasury's monetary accounts are routinely estimated for publication, owing to lags in the reporting of these series. When the actual numbers are eventually received from the Treasury, their inclusion generally involves small revisions to items calculated from these data. The data on reserves of depository institutions and the monetary base are subject to revision for three reasons: errors in data initially reported by depository institutions, revisions to estimates that result from newly-reported quarterly data, and annual revisions to seasonal factors. Most revisions to initially reported data are small, but occasionally a large revision is reported by a depository institution, especially when an unusual event such as a merger occurs. Since the quarterly data are reported with a lag, estimates are constructed using a "surrogate" panel of weekly reporting institutions of similar size and type; these estimates revise once the quarterly data are received. Finally, the seasonal factors are revised annually as the most recent year of data are incorporated into the estimation process.
