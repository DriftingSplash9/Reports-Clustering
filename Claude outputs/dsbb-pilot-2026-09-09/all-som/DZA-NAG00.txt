 2.1.1 Concepts and definitions The general framework used as a reference in compiling the national accounts is the one defined by the United Nations System of National Accounts ( SNA93 ). For purely didactic reasons it also makes reference to the European System of Accounts ( ESA 1995). However, the national accounts are established annually according to the “Système des comptes économiques algériens” (SCEA). It is derived from the system of material product which was previously used in planned economies and differs from the SNA68 and SNA93, since it does not include as productive activity some services such as those from general government, which leads to an overall lower estimate of GDP. 2.3.1 Classification/sectorization The different classifications and nomenclatures used in compiling the national accounts statistics are those defined in SNA93 and by international organizations, in particular the United Nations and the International Monetary Fund (IMF). The institutional units are defined with reference to SNA93 and the institutional sectors are those set out in SNA93, namely nonfinancial corporations, financial corporations, households and sole proprietorships, and government agencies. In some cases, the accounts are adapted to the national context observing international recommendations. For example, this is the case when the institutional classification differentiates between public and private nonfinancial corporations. It is also the case in the classification of transactions when a distinction is made between oil exports and exports of other merchandise to neutralize the weight of the oil sector. Another case that could be cited is the distinction made between formal and informal units of production. For the functional classification of economic activities, the frame of reference is the Algerian Nomenclature of Activities (NAA), which was the subject of an executive decree in 2002. The NAA is perfectly consistent with international classifications, in particular the International Standard Industrial Classification (ISIC, Rev. 3.0) of all economic activities. Similarly, the Algerian Nomenclature of Activities (NAA), which has also been the subject of a decree in 2002, is used for the classification of products. The NAP is very compatible with the United Nations Central Product Classification (CPC, rev. 1.2). From year 2011, the National Statistics Office had developed within the framework of a Maghreb sub-regional partnership two nomenclatures: Activities Nomenclature and Products Nomenclature. The first one is an adaptation of the Standard International Industrial Classification version 4, and the second is an adaptation of the Central Products Classification ( CPC) version 2.0. The consumption expenditure of households is classified in accordance with the Nomenclature of Goods and Services (NBS), the local nomenclature, and the NAP. The consumption expenditure of households is also classified with reference to the Classification of Individual Consumption According to Purpose (COICOP) of the SNA93. Bridge and correspondence tables permit conversions from the NBS to the NAP and to COICOP. For the classification of central government expenditure, and more specifically operating (current) expenses, the ONS uses the Classification of Functions of Government (COFOG), which is defined by both SNA93 and by the IMF Government Finance Statistics Manual ( GFSM 2001). With regard to the compilation of foreign trade statistics, the ONS uses the UN Classification by Broad Economic Categories (CBEC rev. 3). At the product level, foreign trade flows are identified using the Algerian Customs Tariff (TDA). The TDA has eight positions, the first six correspond to the Brussels Harmonized System (HS 2002) and the last two to the Standard International trade Classification (SITC), the 2007 version 4.0 of which is available. Economic operations are defined as recommended by SNA93, making a distinction between transactions in goods and services, distribution operations, and financial transactions. Scope of the data Unit Coverage Unit Coverage The definition of institutional units by the Algerian national accounts generally complies with international standards and definitions and almost all institutional units are covered in the accounts. The nonfinancial corporations sector (NFC) is defined in accordance with SNA93. To meet users’ needs, a distinction is made between public and private NFCs. Quasi-corporations are also represented in the national accounts and are included in the NFCs. All resident NFCs are therefore included in the Algerian national accounts. The government sector presented in the Algerian national accounts, includes central government, administrative public sector agencies, local governments (wilayates and communes) and the various social security funds ( Caisse nationale d’Assurance des Salariés, Caisse nationale de la Retraite, Caisse d’Assurance sociale des Non Salariés, Caisse nationale du Chômage— for employed, retired, self-employed, and unemployed persons). It is therefore possible to comprehensively cover the sector, especially through an annual survey of banks and insurance companies. The activities of the hidden economy are also recorded. 2.4.1 Valuation The national accounts departments of the ONS value gross market production (P.1) at base prices, namely the production price less taxes plus subsidies on goods. Production for our final home use (P.12) is valued at market prices with reference to the prices of similar products (having the same technical characteristics) available on the market. This is the case, for example, all of home consumption of agricultural products, which is recorded both as gross production and final consumption of households. This is also the case for the imputed rents generated by owner occupied households. Nonmarket production is valued at factor cost. This is the case, for example, of houses built by the household occupants themselves (auto-construction). There is a Value Added Tax (VAT) with three rates (0%, 7% and 17%). Gross output is valued at base prices exclusive of billed VAT. However, intermediate consumption is valued at purchase prices (market prices) including non-deductible VAT. The components of demand (intermediate consumption, final consumption and FBCF) do not include the deductible part of VAT. 2.4.2 Recording basis The transactions which are counted in the enterprises balance sheets and which are used to draft the accounts of the not financial companies and finance companies (by means of an Enterprise intermediate system ) are estimated on the basis of the noticed rights (accrual). On the other hand, the operations realized by the public administration (government) are estimated according to the cash basis. Thus, gaps exist between the moment of the realization of the flow and the moment of its recording. However, the national accounting services proceed to certain correctives to data reconciliation of the public funds to the principle of the noticed rights. It is particularly valid for the budgetary receipts with which we try at most the balance sheets data of the companies which are recorded in noticed rights. As an example, the figure relative to the oil tax system registered at the level of the budgetary receipts is replaced by the oil royalty and the Profit tax of Companies (PTC) or more exactly the Oil Direct Tax which are directly refunding from the balance sheets of the companies of hydrocarbons sectors that are in noticed rights. Besides, the user of the national accounts remains well informed on various procedures and modes of registration of flows through the method documents. 2.4.3 Grossing/netting procedures Transactions between production units or establishments of the same enterprise are valued on a gross basis. 3.1.1 Source data collection programs The national accounts departments use data sources from either the ONS or the sectors. There are no problems accessing the data produced by the ONS (statistics on production, prices, employment, etc.). Furthermore, at fairly regular intervals and in conjunction with the national accounts departments, survey statisticians update and refresh the different statistical surveys to take account of national accounts needs and of changes in the country’s economic and social situation. It is thought that the available sources of infomation meet national accounts needs, with very few exceptions. The sampling plan guarantees that the population of enterprises is properly represented. Indeed, enterprises are broken down into two broad categories, which are surveyed annually: enterprises (public or private) with 10 or more salaried employees, all of which are surveyed; enterprises with less than 10 salaried employees, which are surveyed by sampling. The sample of enterprises is formed taking a number of criteria into account, including activity, size (turnover, employees) and location. The stratification used allows for both regional coverage and coverage by corporate activity. Owing to the movement of enterprises, in particular very small ones, these samples are reviewed every year. The survey questionnaires are compiled by a multidisciplinary team that includes, inter alia, survey statisticians, national accounts compilers, and information technology specialists. It is done in such a way as to meet a list of requirements covering the needs of different users. For the most part, the list reflects national accounts needs. It should be noted that whenever a new base year is selected, these questionnaires are revised and updated to take account of new needs and new instruments (nomenclatures, concepts, valuation methods, etc.) and to adapt them to the reality on the ground, which may change from one period to the next. The IT teams are involved in the management and application of the questionnaires. Data input programs are devised by the ONS for the different sections of the questionnaire, accompanied by a battery of tests (for data entry errors, assurances of reasonableness, overall consistency, etc.) and output statements. The statistical surveys of enterprises by the ONS offer Algerian national accounts compilers statistical information at very detailed levels of the data. The data collected through questionnaires associated with corporate accounting documents collected during the survey help determine national accounts aggregates, in particular the main goods and services transactions (gross output, intermediate consumption, gross fixed capital formation). More specifically, these data are used in supply-use balances by product [and] the accounts for branches of activity (production and operating accounts). An intermediate enterprise system (SIE) is prepared by the national accounts departments of the ONS. This system makes it possible to transfer operations from corporate accounts (business accounting) to the national accounts aggregates for the nonfinancial corporations (NFC) and financial corporations (FC) sectors. Household surveys are regularly carried out to determine income and expenses. Indeed, there are several household surveys, some regular and others occasional, such as the Living Standards Measurement Study (LSMS) of households. Every 10 years, the ONS conducts a survey of household consumption expenditure (the budget survey). In addition to aspects related to household consumption, this survey has three very detailed sections on household income. Increasing the frequency of this survey to every five years is currently being considered. Similarly, the ONS conducts a quarterly household survey of employment, which also has a very detailed section on household income. The base from which the household sample is drawn is the general file, which is compiled after each General Population and Housing Census (RGPH) and is regularly updated. The household samples are compiled using universally accepted techniques and sampling errors are known in advance with confidence intervals. The sampling is random, probabilistic and three-stage. The samples selected automatically through systematic drawings (with one step) are representative of households by region and size. The samples are regularly revised. The household surveys conducted by the ONS do take account of seasonal variations that could affect household expenditure (composition and levels) as well as employment and income. In particular, this is the case of the month of Ramadan, when Muslim households in Algeria fast. Coverage is exhaustive for “ordinary” resident households. Group and nomadic households are not covered by these surveys. This fact is clearly stated in the methodological documents on household surveys. However, this does not affect the quality of the survey data. On the contrary, these categories of populations are covered by the general population and housing censuses. As the surveys are often nationwide in scope, the samples selected are representative of the entire geographic territory. The compilation of questionnaires and other survey templates meets the relevant standards. A multidisciplinary team is responsible for compiling the questionnaire based on the various needs and on survey priorities and objectives. Pilot surveys are systematically conducted in the field to test the overall consistency of the questionnaire and a proper understanding of all the questions so that there is no ambiguity or misunderstanding. These surveys are always conducted by a corps of survey personnel (university level) who are well trained, thereby ensuring an excellent rapport between the survey giver and the survey taker and consequently the quality of the data collected. The content and structure of the questionnaire are not fixed in time. At regular intervals, they are revised to take account of new and pressing needs on the ground. The entire data processing chain, from the data collected in the questionnaire to the output or the results is computerized to ensure that quality information with minimal errors is available in a relatively short period time. Access to the fiscal statistics poses no problem. Detailed information on central government is available annually. However, financial data at the local level are available only as aggregates. At present, no capital stock data are available. However, estimates of capital stock are made by the national accounting departments using the perpetual inventory method (PIM). 3.1.2 Source data definitions, scope, classifications, valuation, and time of recording Generally speaking, core data used as inputs in compiling the national accounts are sufficiently close to the framework prepared by SNA93 in terms of concepts and definitions, valuation methods, classification, etc. For example, data plucked from surveys conducted by the ONS (of households or enterprises) cover these requirements perfectly well. The same can be said of administrative sources like the balance of payments, foreign trade statistics, and government finance statistics. For some very specific aspects where these sources do not meet certain standards, corrections or adjustments must be made to the source data by the national accounts departments before they can be used. For example, an overall adjustment is made to imports to express them at f.o.b. instead of c.i.f. prices. Some fiscal operations are processed so that they can be recorded on an accrual instead of a cash basis. As for the reference period, the period covered by the source data coincides with the one used in the national accounts (the calendar year). One exception, however, is the agricultural statistics, but the crop year is generally taken to be synonymous with the calendar year. We estimate that the coverage of activities in terms of value added is accurate. As an illustration, the public sector (including the oil sector), which accounts for some 65 percent of overall value added, is covered in its entirety. If we add the private sector, which is covered through sampling, overall coverage is easily greater than 90 percent. In addition, our statistical surveys are well representative of the branches (as defined in the ISIC) within the sectors of activity, and are sufficiently representative of the products (in the sense of the CPC) within the branches. 3.1.3 Source data timeliness Generally speaking, the core data are obtained on time and in accordance with the data collection schedule drawn up by the ONS. This is particularly true for the applications (surveys), which are exclusively under the responsibility of the ONS and whose deadlines are being met. That notwithstanding, some information not produced by the ONS is somewhat late in arriving. The ONS always tries to keep a close relationship with producers of the core data and uses every means to remind enterprises or sectors that are late in reporting the requested information. 3.3.1 Source data statistical techniques The data processing procedure is correct The core data used by the Algerian national accounts compilers are compiled at different stages so as to minimize errors. To do so, procedures were implemented to guarantee the consistency and relevance of the data before they are used. These procedures make it possible to detect errors due to double counting or omissions, calculation mistakes, errors in units of measure, coding errors, etc. In some situations, these procedures even make it possible to formulate an opinion on the presumption of existing informal activities in certain branches. Statistics with high levels of flagrant inconsistencies are never used to compile the national accounts, especially if these biases could seriously affect the quality of the accounts. In this case, the information is either rejected or corrected using appropriate and sound methods and procedures. Appropriate measures are taken to validate the core data Core data are validated using a multiplicity of measures. Each data item, irrespective of its origin (source) is meticulously tested, checked, and filtered before use, to better determine its consistency and relevance. To illustrate: The information is checked against other similar information produced by other sources (benchmarking). For example, determining whether the magnitude of the uses reported in the household survey is close to the amount advanced in the enterprise survey. The information is correlated with other variables to observe its behavior. For example, the following correlations are studied: between commercial margins and marketable resources and between household final consumption and household income. Statistical analysis (averages, standard deviation, confidence intervals, etc.). The data is presented in time series to see its general trend over time (indices, population, etc.). Composition, distribution, and growth ratios are calculated. In addition to these techniques, another interesting way to make pronouncements on and validate the data is the close relationship of Algeria’s national accounts compilers with data providers. Working meetings between the two parties provide qualitative indications about the nature of the information produced, the context, the tools used (nomenclatures, valuation methods, concepts and definitions, etc.), versions, scope, etc. Sometimes certain information is not available or does not arrive on time. In such cases, the national accounts compilers reconstitute the data. Sound techniques are used based on hypotheses that are generally verified in the field. One basic principle is observed: certain key data must never be estimated. If, for example, the company responsible for producing hydrocarbons fails to respond, its information must be obtained. 3.3.2 Other statistical procedures The proper adjustments are made to the core data to make sure that they comply with the requirements of the national accounts. Procedures were developed by the national accounts departments to include in the assessment of GDP and its associated aggregates, the activities related to the hidden economy (illegal, underground and informal activities), solely for the purpose of ensuring that the coverage of the national accounts is comprehensive. Owing to the nature of the activities related to the hidden economy, there are no preset or universal methods its handling. The specificities related to each branch or sector of activity covered in the hidden economy, the available information sources, their frequency and their quality, require national accounts compilers to use solutions that may not be the same for the different branches of activity. Sometimes, within the same branch (e.g. construction, public works, and housing), several approaches may be juxtaposed to have the best guarantee of the quality of the results obtained, even if it is clear that the phenomena to be captured are, by definition, difficult to quantify. By institutional sector, the hidden economy covers the household sector (sole proprietorships), in particular, and to a lesser extent private nonfinancial enterprises. The government and financial institutions sectors have little or no connection with the hidden economy (except for the underground economy, for statistical purposes). The Algerian national accounts compilers try as best they can to include in the calculation of GDP the share of activity in the hidden economy. These corrections are made at two levels First, there is an ex-ante adjustment made to branches or sectors of activity (the bottom-up approach) before any consolidation work is done. The results obtained undergo a series of consistency tests to determine their relevance: observation of general trends, application of the Franz method, observation of the resulting balance in certain cases, comparison of intermediate consumption rates between the public and private sectors, particularly the rates for the hidden economy, etc. Secondly, consistency tests are performed on the overall economy resulting in further ex-post corrections (top-down approach) if there is the presumption of error. For example, the three approaches to GDP are observed, a comparison is made between financing capacity (or requirement) in the accumulation account and the transactions account with the rest of the world. The valuation of production and other goods and services transactions is sufficiently detailed. When a new accounting base is introduced, an economic census is performed to capture production at the product level (6-digit level). However, products are aggregated at an initial level of activity (4 digits) to obtain the classes. These classes are in turn aggregated into three groups (3 digits) and the groups are further condensed into two divisions (2 digits). Intermediate consumption is estimated at a sufficiently fine level of detail. The level of detail of intermediate consumption exactly parallels that of production based on the tree given above. The detailed surveys of structure which are realized by NSO at regular intervals allow to set out intermediate consumptions by product. The use of ratios determined on the basis of benchmarking techniques or other sources is monitored. In estimating value added, Algerian national accounts compilers make reference to certain ratios, in particular technical production ratios. Each time the accounting base is changed, these ratios are recalculated using the economic surveys of enterprises and all their establishments (units of production). Within the same accounting base, these ratios are also updated from one year to another. We already have the accounting documents from corporations that enable us to update the ratios. We also know the intermediate consumption figures for many branches. For the agricultural sector, for example, we have the intermediate consumption figures by product (plants and seeds, fertilizer, plant health products, insecticide, livestock feed, energy products; etc.). For electricity and gas products, for example, we know the consumer branches. Appropriate statistical techniques are applied to particular issues regarding the calculation of GDP. Owner-occupied housing. In addition to the rents actually paid by tenants for housing, imputed (notional) rents are also calculated for purposes of international comparability. To do so Algerian national accounts compilers use data from the survey of household consumption expenditure. Outside the survey years, the Algerian national accounts compilers prepare estimates using the updated structure of all national housing by status of occupant (owner, tenant, etc.) and the market prices of rents. Stock valuation adjustments. In valuing stocks, a distinction is made between producer stock, user stock, and business stock. However, owing to the lack of available information, no adjustments are currently made to attribute a share of any gains or losses to stocks. The same can be said of intermediate consumption. Consumption of fixed capital. Most of the aggregates and account balances are expressed as gross amounts, thereby avoiding the tricky problem of valuing fixed capital consumption. For the nonfinancial and sole proprietorships sector, sometimes the fiscal depreciation reported in companies’ balance sheets used as a rough estimate. We are aware that this is not economic depreciation and has no direct or indirect impact on GDP. For the government sector, we actually use the perpetual inventory method (PIM) to value the stock of capital and fixed capital consumption. Long backward looking series are reconstituted dating back to 1963 and all investment flows are revalued as new using the appropriate price indices. Cash or accrual based accounting. A noted earlier, the fiscal statistics currently available are expressed by the “income-outlay” formula. The same can be said for the balance of payments, which is not totally compiled on an accrual basis. However, Algerian national accounts compilers try to make some adjustments to source data to move them closer to the accrual principle. In addition, corporate balance sheets are among the most common and preferred sources of statistical information. These balance sheets are prepared on an accrual basis. From there, we can conclude that taxes and subsidies on products are valued on an accrual basis as well, because they are directly drawn from these balance sheets. Furthermore, a large share of public revenue is valued on an accrual basis as many taxes have been so adjusted. By contrast, public expenditure is closer to a cash basis. Appropriate methods are used to calculate GDP by volume. Double deflation is used to deflate value added. Intermediate production and consumption are deflated separately at a sufficiently detailed level. Measurements in volume of taxes/subsidies on products. Taxes and subsidies in volume are obtained by applying base year tax rates. GDP is also valued using the expenditure approach. The available sources of information allow the components of GDP to be valued separately. This is particularly true of household consumption, gross fixed capital formation, and changes in stocks. Household final consumption expenditure is broken down using a variety of classifications: according to the Nomenclature of Goods and Services (NBS—the ONS’ internal nomenclature) to the 900 level; the International Standard Industrial Classification at the greatest level of detail; the Central Product Classification at the greatest level of detail; the Classification of Individual Consumption According to Purpose (COICOP) at its greatest level of detail (four digits). Government’s final consumption expenditure is also broken down as much as possible: according to the International Standard Industrial Classification at its most detailed level; according to the Central Product Classification at the most detailed level; and according to the Classification of Functions of Government (COFOG) at its most detailed level (five digits). Gross fixed capital formation and changes in stocks are also broken down at detailed levels of activity. Very rarely are fixed ratios used in valuing the components of demand. For the treatment of specific issues related to GDP valuation, appropriate techniques are used: the final expenditure of government agencies excluding accessory or residual sales (university publications, etc.). These latter are transferred to their respective market branches through the production matrix; spending abroad by Algerian residents is included in imports and household final consumption. A territorial correction line is planned in the Algerian Supply and Use Table; expenditure in our territory by nonresidents is considered to be exports and therefore excluded from the final consumption of resident households; objects of value are not assessed. In volume, the components of GDP are valued using proper methods and by means of appropriate indices: Each component of GDP is deflated by an appropriate price index. Deflation is at an appreciable level of detail. Household final consumption is deflated by product at a very detailed level using core consumer price indices. Ultimately, the composite index implicit in final consumption is very close to the CPI. The components of production costs are deflated for government consumption expenditure. The methods used to deflate changes in stocks are not completely satisfactory. Quarterly accounts are compiled in accordance with international methodologies, in particular with reference to the IMF manual on quarterly accounts. The Denton method is followed in benchmarking quarterly data to annual data. The core data used in the quarterly accounts are gross and are not corrected for any seasonal or business day variations. 3.4.1 Validation of intermediate results Systematic cross-checks of various data make it possible to validate intermediate results. These cross-checks are performed when the same variable can be obtained from several sources (household employment survey and corporate employment survey, ONS industrial production survey, and Ministry of Industry industrial production survey, statistics on oil and gas exports reported by the Directorate General of Customs with those provided by the Ministry of Energy and Mines, etc.). The sources are validated by establishing a hierarchy of independent sources and through a battery of tests of consistency and reasonableness for the same source. Knowledge of the methodologies of each source (objectives, units observed, coverage, response rate, nomenclatures used, etc.) is also an important element of judgment in validating the intermediate results. The compilation of consistent and homogenous long series helps validate the information. 3.4.2 Assessment of intermediate data Before they are used, the intermediate data are carefully examined and tested. Procedures for control and evaluation of the quality of the data are systematically implemented. These procedures detect any irregularities or inconsistencies in the database. If these inconsistencies are noted, the proper adjustments are made to neutralize them. 3.4.3 Assessment of discrepancies and other problems in statistical outputs The analysis of statistical inconsistencies is performed at three levels. At the microeconomic level , where the basic and individual information is carefully analyzed through a battery of consistency and reasonableness tests. If required, corrections or adjustments are made at that level. At the mesoeconomic level , another analysis is made of the data that was already processed and aggregated at an intermediate level. The supply-use table is one of the tools used to analyze any inconsistencies by preparing highly detailed product balances. This shows the reasonableness of SUTs by product. Similarly, there is a statement of the reasonableness of the branch accounts. At the macroeconomic level , the last analysis is done after final consolidation when the tradeoffs are being made between the three GDP approaches (production, expenditure, and income). In this case, even if there are gaps between the three approaches to GDP, they do not appear explicitly as statistical gaps or adjustments for errors and omissions because these differences are systematically absorbed into the components of GDP. Furthermore, certain variables are controlled with respect to some work at the sectoral level (ministries) either at the university level or by public or private research firms. Similarly, sometimes we take into account estimates or projections made by international institutions (IMF, World Bank, etc.), known from their professionalism and know-how. We do take them into account but this should not influence our approach, which must be consistent (principle of permanent methods) and objective. 3.5.1 Revision studies and analyses Upon review of the base year, internal studies are conducted to evaluate the differences between the data of the old base year and those of the new base year. A good share of the differences is imputable to different concepts, nomenclatures, valuation methods between the two bases. The sources of any other discrepancies, which are marginal, are investigated. There are usually three explanations: either errors or biases in the sources used, which were not detected when the accounts were compiled; or errors in calculation; or using the wrong methodology, which is not a fit for the variable estimated. It would appear that all these aspects are taken into account when the accounts for the same base year are reviewed, or at the time of the change of base year. When the errors or biases are directly imputable to the source of the data used, steps are taken with the entities responsible for that source and discussions initiated to determine the exact cause of the error. In almost all the cases, the sectors are unaware that these errors or biases exist. Concrete measures are then taken by the sector to remedy the problem. Calculation errors are easy to correct when they are properly identified. In some cases, the approaches used are responsible for the errors or biases (valuation of changes in stocks, valuation of government fixed capital consumption by the MIP, imputed rents, etc.). In these cases, the methodologies are revised and adapted to the variables studied and available data sources. 4.3.3 Dissemination of revision studies and analyses National accounts users have access to the economic accounts once they are available. The accounts that are published provisionally are revised so that they become semi-final or final. The source of the differences is explained in the methodological notes and the metadata (essentially because the basic data are provisional then revised, and also because of the more comprehensive sources used in the final accounts). However, the differences in the figures between the various versions are not currently analyzed for the public. It is clear that the more we advance in the versions of the accounts for a single year (provisional to semi-final to final) the more the relevance, reliability and details of the accounts are improved. However, it is difficult to find objective solutions to the dilemma: rapidly available accounts that are not based on comprehensive sources, or better quality accounts that arrive late. 5.1.5 Dissemination on request The national accounts statistics are routinely disseminated upon completion. It is clear that the dissemination media cannot contain all the available data, therefore any additional information is provided upon request. Specific and menu-based requests are also addressed to the ONS national accounts departments. All requests are granted unless there is a problem with the availability of the requested information. There is no charge for additional information supplied from the menu (it is for the public’use).
