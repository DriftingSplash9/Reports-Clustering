 2.1.1 Concepts and definitions The accounts of the banking sector rely on balance sheet data for banks and other depository institutions. Money stock, domestic credit, and the external position are estimated directly. There is no attempt to recast the data in the form of a monetary or banking survey to show how monetary and credit aggregates can be derived as the residuals of balance sheet items for the sector as a whole. Two monetary aggregates are published for the United States. M1 consists of (1) currency outside the U.S. Treasury, Federal Reserve Banks, and the vaults of depository institutions; (2) travelers checks of nonbank issuers; (3) demand deposits at all commercial banks other than those due to depository institutions, the U.S. government, and foreign banks and official institutions, less cash items in the process of collection and Federal Reserve float; and (4) other checkable deposits (OCDs), consisting of negotiable order of withdrawal (NOW) and automatic transfer service (ATS) accounts at depository institutions, credit union share draft accounts and demand deposits at thrift institutions. M2 consists of M1 plus savings deposits (including money market deposit accounts), small-denomination time deposits (time deposits-including retail RPs-in amounts of less than $100,000), and balances in retail money market mutual funds. M2 excludes individual retirement account (IRA) and Keogh balances at depository institutions and money market funds. The concepts and definitions are consistent with Monetary and Financial Statistics Manual and Compilation Guide (Washington, D.C: IMF, 2016). 2.3.1 Classification/sectorization At the weekly frequency of the H.8 statistical release, sectorization of the domestic accounts is not complete. Credit to general government is measured by U.S. government securities plus state and local government securities and loans to state and local governments at all commercial banks. State and local government securities and loans to state and local governments at all commercial banks are included with data on credit to the rest of the domestic economy. These amounts are available on a quarterly frequency from Call Reports. State and local government securities and loans to state and local governments at U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banks are not reported separately and are included with data on credit to the rest of the domestic economy. Any direct loans to nonfinancial public enterprises would be included with data on credit to the rest of the domestic economy. The Treasury International Capital (TIC) data are the basis for the external position of the banking system. Sectorization of these accounts is done by residency of the account holder or customer as the basic criterion for distinguishing "foreign" or "foreigner." This criterion is consistent with the IMF's Balance of Payments Manual, Sixth Edition (BPM6). Specifically, "foreign" or foreigner" includes: (1) Individuals, including U.S. citizens, residing outside the U.S. and its overseas territories and possessions; (2) Entities that file an IRS Form W-9 indicating that the individual or entity is a nonresident alien or foreign entity not subject to certain U.S. information return reporting or to backup withholding rules; (3) Branches, subsidiaries and other affiliates of U.S.-incorporated entities located outside the U.S.; (4) Foreign governments and any subdivision, agency or instrumentality thereof, including all foreign official nonbanking institutions, even if located in the U.S. (e.g., an embassy, consulate, or other diplomatic establishment of a foreign country), and (5) Official international or regional organizations, and affiliated agencies thereof, created by treaty or convention between sovereign states, even if located in the U.S., such as the World Bank, the IMF, and the United Nations. Scope of the data The banking sector in the United States (also referred to as the depository sector) consists of commercial banks and thrift institutions, with the latter group containing savings banks, savings and loan associations, and credit unions. At the end of 2024 there were roughly 9,000 depository institutions, including U.S. commercial banks; branches or agencies of foreign-chartered banks, or Edge Act and agreement corporations; savings institutions; and credit unions. The monetary aggregates include currency (outside the U.S. Treasury, Federal Reserve Banks, and the vaults of depository institutions), a substantial amount of which is held outside the United States by foreign individuals, firms, and governments. Liabilities of depository institutions that go into the monetary aggregates are those at offices located in the 50 states and the District of Columbia. Deposit liabilities at other offices, including those of depository institutions in territories and possessions of the United States, are excluded from M1 and M2. The monetary aggregates also include close substitutes for liabilities of depository institutions in the U.S. and, therefore, cover some institutions other than depository institutions. At the M1 level, travelers' checks of nonbank issuers are included. At the M2 level, balances in retail money market mutual funds (those funds primarily serving households) are included. Bank credit, as shown on Federal Reserve Statistical Release H.8, covers credit extended by the following types of institutions in the 50 states and the District of Columbia: U.S.-chartered commercial banks, U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banks, and Edge Act and Agreement corporations. Bank credit excludes offshore offices of U.S.-chartered commercial banks and International Banking Facilities (IBFs). Bank credit also excludes credit extended at offices of U.S.-chartered banks in territories and possessions of the United States. The central bank is not part of the analytical accounts of the banking sector, and, thus, its assets are not part of bank credit. Thrift credit (available quarterly in Federal Reserve Statistical Release Z.1), covers credit extended by savings banks, savings and loan associations, and credit unions located in the 50 states and the District of Columbia. The external position of the banking sector is calculated from a separate data flow (the Treasury International Capital (TIC) data) and covers a slightly broader range of institutions than bank and thrift credit. All types of depository institutions in the 50 states and the District of Columbia are included, plus IBFs, bank holding companies, and some brokers and dealers in securities. The TIC data exclude positions in securities with maturities greater than one year. Transactions (flows) in such securities are recorded in the TIC system, but the positions (stocks) are not reported. Exceptions to coverage The deposits of depository institutions that are being liquidated are excluded from the monetary aggregates. Since 1991, the federal bank regulators have been directed to find a resolution for failing banks that results in the least cost to the federal deposit insurance fund. Often this means arranging a merger with another bank. In some cases, though, a failing bank may be liquidated. At that point, the chartering authority revokes the charter, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation is appointed as receiver, and the bank's assets and liabilities leave the reporting systems that underlie the analytical accounts of the banking sector. 2.4.1 Valuation Most bank and thrift balance sheet data used to be valued at historical cost or the lower of cost or market. Trading assets and liabilities were the only items routinely marked to market. In the past decade, however, the accounting profession has been pushing banks further toward market-value accounting. Most of the securities holdings of banks are marked to market. Positions in off-balance-sheet derivatives are also marked to market, and the resulting market value is carried on the balance sheet as an asset (if positive) or a liability (if negative). Other asset and liability items are generally not marked to market. A large part of the monetary aggregates consists of liabilities of depository institutions that, in general, are not marked to market. Loan and lease assets are generally reported at book value before deductions for loan and lease losses. The allowance for loan and lease losses is not deducted from bank credit or thrift credit, but it is subtracted in arriving at total assets for depository institutions. Nonperforming assets are included in bank credit and thrift credit. The amounts of such assets are monitored using quarterly Call Reports (see section IV below). For the external position of the banking system, TIC respondents are instructed to report claims and liabilities positions on a gross basis at the face amount of the instrument. Accrued interest is reported if past due or credited to another account on the reporters' books. Claims are reported net of any recognized charge-offs. The reporting system underlying the monetary aggregates attempts to exclude any deposits denominated in foreign currencies. The main report (see the discussion of the FR 2900 in section IV below) includes these deposits, converted to U.S. dollars. A separate report (FR 2915) records, on a quarterly basis, the amount of deposits denominated in foreign currencies at depository institutions in the U.S., also converted to U.S. dollars. These balances are then excluded from the U.S. money stock. The amounts involved have proved to be negligible, accounting for less than 0.1 percent of M1 and M2. Foreign-currency deposits are converted to U.S. dollars using one of two options: The reporter can use an exchange rate for the first day of the relevant reporting period, or it can use the exchange rate prevailing for each day of the reporting period. Regardless of which option is elected, the exchange rates to be used for this conversion are either the 10:00 a.m. rates quoted for major currencies by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, or the noon buying rates certified by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York for customs purposes, or some other consistent series of exchange rate quotations. The reporting system underlying bank credit uses a similar approach to translating balance sheet items denominated in foreign currencies into U.S. dollars. The system tracks balance sheets for panels of U.S.-chartered banks each Wednesday. Reporting banks have the option of using the exchange rate prevailing on the day before the report date or the actual report date. The exchange rates used must be either the 10:00 a.m. rates quoted for major currencies by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, or the noon buying rates certified by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York for customs purposes, or some other consistent series of exchange rate quotations. Unlike the monetary aggregates, however, no data are collected on the amounts of various items that are denominated in foreign currencies, and these amounts, therefore, are not separated out. TIC respondents are instructed to convert transactions or positions payable in foreign currencies into U.S. dollar equivalents using the spot exchange rates at the close of business on the as-of-date of the particular report. No data are reported in foreign currency units. 3.1.1 Source data collection programs The principal source of information for the monetary aggregates is a report of deposits and vault cash known as the FR 2900. Large depository institutions file this daily 14-item report each week; small ones file it for one week each quarter. Very small depository institutions file a 2-item deposits report (FR 2910a) annually or no deposits report. The boundary lines between these four categories of reporting are based on total deposits and total reservable liabilities and change over time with the growth of each of these two aggregates. All depository institutions are reviewed each year to determine what panel they should be in for the next reporting year (September to September). In the spring, Call Reports (see next paragraph) are used to determine if institutions not currently filing a deposits report should file the FR 2910a. In the summer, data from the FR 2910a and data from the existing panels of FR 2900 reporters are used to reassign depository institutions to the appropriate panel, if necessary. The FR 2910a, which measures total deposits and total reservable liabilities, is a screening device used in the assignment of depository institutions to a reporting panel. It does not have enough deposit detail to be used in constructing the money stock. For institutions on the FR 2910a panel, and for institutions that do not file any deposits report, Call Report data on deposits are used for money stock construction. For institutions that file the weekly or quarterly FR 2900, those data are used in money stock construction. Additional sources are used for particular nondeposit components of the monetary aggregates. Currency in circulation comes from daily condition statements of the Federal Reserve Banks. Balances in money market mutual funds are obtained from the Investment Company Institute which collects the data and shares it with the Federal Reserve. Data from a quarterly report (FR 2502q) collects liabilities for foreign offices of larger banks. In addition, data from the Bank of England and the Bank of Canada on U.S. dollar-denominated liabilities of banks in those countries to U.S. residents are used. Bank credit relies on one weekly report that collects balance-sheet information from commercial banks on a single-day (Wednesday) basis. This report generally collects a subset of the information provided by all banks each quarter on their Call Reports. The FR 2644 report collects a detailed balance sheet from hundreds of U.S.-chartered banks and U.S. agencies and branches of foreign banks. The reporting panel is redrawn approximately every 3 years, so that the panel of U.S.-chartered banks covers around 50 percent of total banking assets at offices in the U.S. The panel of U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banks is drawn judgmentally to obtain adequate coverage of the wholesale lending activities of these institutions and adequate representation by country. An additional source of bank credit information is the quarter-end data from Call Reports (FFIEC 031, 041, 002). These reports are used to benchmark the samples of smaller U.S.-chartered banks and U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banks and to perform inter-series edits of data quality (see section V. below). Thrift credit is estimated from Call Reports filed by savings banks and savings and loan associations(FFIEC 031, 041) and credit unions (NCUA 5300) on a quarterly basis. The data are single-day observations for the ends of calendar quarters. The external position of the banking system, reported through the Treasury International Capital (TIC) system, is based on certain thresholds. All banks, other depository institutions, bank or financial holding companies, and registered broker-dealers located in the United States, including agencies and branches of foreign-based banks, are required to submit monthly, quarterly, and semi-annual reports of all covered claims and liabilities vis-a-vis foreigners once the reportable positions meet or exceed established thresholds. These thresholds are either $50 million or more in the aggregate or $25 million or more with respect to any individual country. Once a reporting entity meets or exceeds the reporting threshold in any specified reporting period, the entity must continue to submit forms for each subsequent reporting period of that year and for the following year, regardless of the amounts. The external positions of the banking system, as reported in TIC data, exclude balances arising from banks' issuance of, or investment in, securities with an original maturity of more than 1 year. These are defined as long-term securities and are reported on a transactions basis on a monthly frequency. 3.1.3 Source data timeliness FR 2900 figures are daily data are for each day of the reporting week. Call Report figures are single-day observations taken at the end of March, June, September, and December. All federally-insured depository institutions file a Call Report with their federal regulators. These quarterly reports, which consist of a balance sheet, an income statement and supporting schedules, generally contain more detail than higher-frequency reports used to construct monetary aggregates and bank credit, but do not contain all the items necessary to construct the money stock as currently defined. 3.3.1 Source data statistical techniques Most of the reports that underlie the analytical accounts of the banking sector are collected by the Federal Reserve Banks from depository institutions in their area. The data are edited for validity and quality, and the process of checking edit failures is begun. At the same time, the individual responses are sent electronically to the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and are updated on a flow basis. These data are stored at the Board of Governors in electronic data archives. Micro archival data are then aggregated in various ways to produce the macro data that make up the analytical accounts of the banking sector. Source data for the monetary aggregates are taken as reported and added up. There are no sample blowups involved. In any reporting period missing data for a particular respondent are estimated using the best judgment of data analysts at the Federal Reserve Banks and the Board of Governors. Because the FR 2900 is used to calculate reserve requirements, it is a required report, and very few institutions neglect to file in any reporting period. Banks that are merging, and may be in the process of combining computer systems, however, sometimes have difficulty reporting on the correct, consolidated basis in a timely manner. In such cases, staff at the Reserve Banks may estimate the data until the respondent is confident that it can produce good numbers. Bank credit data for large U.S.-chartered banks are taken as reported and added up. Data for the samples of smaller U.S.-chartered banks and U.S. branches and agencies of foreign banks are blown up to represent their respective universes. Blow-up factors are obtained from Call Reports. The ratio of the universe to the sample is calculated for each item, and those ratios are used to blow up the weekly sample data until the next quarterly Call Report is available. Changes to the structure of the banking system, such as mergers, closures, new banks, etc., can have distorting effects on the universe estimates derived from samples. Partly for this reason, the Federal Reserve System closely tracks changes in banking structure and makes adjustments to the sample blowups to get the best possible estimate for bank credit each week. Missing data for any reporting period are estimated by Federal Reserve Bank staff and Board staff using their best judgment. Thrift credit is observed at the end of each quarter. The data are taken as reported and added up. These data are collected by other federal agencies and are not under the control of the Federal Reserve System. Nonetheless, similar editing and estimation procedures are conducted by these other federal agencies. As noted above, the TIC data on the external position of the banking system consist of a set of monthly quarterly, and semi-annual reports for the universe of all banks, plus some other institutions, such as bank holding companies and broker-dealers. No blow-ups are involved. If a respondent's TIC data are missing for a particular reporting period, the prior period's data are substituted until the actual data can be included in the database. This is a rare event because respondents are closely monitored for timely and complete reporting. 3.3.2 Other statistical procedures Major components of the monetary aggregates are seasonally adjusted separately and are listed on Federal Reserve Statistical Release H.6. Seasonally adjusted M1 or M2 is the sum of the relevant seasonally adjusted components. Bank credit is treated in a similar fashion, with the major seasonally adjusted components listed on Federal Reserve Statistical Release H.8. Thrift credit is seasonally adjusted as part of the flow of funds accounts. Data on the external position of the banking system are not seasonally adjusted. 3.4.1 Validation of intermediate results As mentioned above, most of the underlying data for the analytical accounts of the banking sector are verified through a data editing system. Once the data are in electronic form, validity edits are performed, for example, to see that numeric items contain numbers rather than letters, or that balance sheet items are nonnegative. Quality edits consider the change in the value of an item since the last time it was reported. Large (absolute value) dollar or percentages changes are flagged for further review. The tolerances for quality edits are set using empirical distributions of changes and can be modified as experience is gained with particular reporters or groups of reporters. Data analysts at the Board of Governors and at the Federal Reserve Banks pursue instances of apparent failures in validity and quality edits, and try to obtain corrections or explanations from reporters. 3.4.3 Assessment of discrepancies and other problems in statistical outputs Another type of quality edit is performed when the same, or similar, item is reported on two different data flows. For example, commercial and industrial loans at banks appear on the weekly (Wednesday) FR 2644 and on the quarter-end Call Report (FFIEC 031, 041, 002). For a particular bank, values for the Wednesday nearest the quarter-end can be compared with quarterly data within some tolerance. These inter-series edits are an important part of data verification, but they are not available for every item. The TIC micro data are subjected to automatic edits for consistency and reasonableness. Where possible, the individual submissions are compared to similar data items reported by banks and bank holding companies on Federal Reserve System regulatory reports. Intra-series and inter-series edit failures are reviewed by analysts at the Federal Reserve Banks. Respondents are contacted and explanations, or corrected data, are obtained. These data corrections may extend back as far as the beginning of the previous calendar year. None
