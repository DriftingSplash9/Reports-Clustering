 2.1.1 Concepts and definitions Concepts and definitions Foreign currency liquidity is the difference between official foreign currency resources and net short-term forward foreign currency commitments. It represents the value of foreign currency that a country's authorities could deploy for policy purposes over the subsequent 12 months. Official reserve assets are external assets that are readily available to and controlled by the monetary authorities for meeting balance of payments financing needs, for intervention in exchange markets to affect the currency exchange rate, and for other related purposes. In the case of Australia, these include foreign currency-denominated asset holdings, gold bullion, SDRs, the reserve position in the IMF, and other reserve assets. Other reserve assets include the market value of derivatives with non-resident entities, holdings in the Asian Bond Fund, loans to the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT), and holdings in the deposit and loan accounts of the IMF's Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST). Relationship to internationally accepted guidelines International reserves and foreign currency liquidity data are compiled on the basis of definitions contained in the sixth edition of the Balance of Payments and International Investment Position Manual and the International Reserves and Foreign Currency Liquidity – Guidelines for a Data Template ( https://www.imf.org/external/np/sta/ir/IRProcessWeb/pdf/guide2013.pdf ). 2.3.1 Classification/sectorization The IMF's international reserves and foreign currency liquidity framework comprises official foreign assets and short-term forward foreign currency commitments. Official foreign assets are classified into official reserve assets and other foreign currency assets. Official reserve assets include foreign exchange, monetary gold, Special Drawing Rights, the reserve position in the IMF, and other reserve assets. Foreign exchange include bonds and notes, treasury bills, deposits with commercial banks, deposits with foreign monetary authorities, and repurchase agreements. Other reserve assets include investments in the Asian bond funds, the market value of derivatives with non-residents, loans to the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust, and holdings in the deposit and loan accounts of the IMF's Resilience and Sustainability Trust. Other foreign currency assets include certain foreign currency claims on domestic residents and foreign currency borrowed under the RBA's bilateral swap agreements with other central banks. Short-term forward foreign currency commitments include future inflows and outflows of foreign currency that will affect official foreign currency assets over the next 12 months. In the case of the RBA, these include notional values of unsettled spot and forward foreign exchange transactions vis-á-vis the Australian dollar as well as future maturities of the RBA's outstanding gold loans, gold swaps, repurchase agreements and transactions under the RBA's bilateral local currency swap agreements with other central banks. 2.2.1 Scope The IMF's international reserves and foreign currency liquidity framework comprises official foreign assets and short-term forward foreign currency commitments. Scope of the data Institutional coverage: The RBA controls all of Australia's international reserves and foreign currency liquidity holdings, with the exception of the reserve position in the IMF and a share of Australia's holdings of SDRs, which are assets of the Australian Government. Instrument coverage: All international reserves and foreign currency liquidity assets, including short-term forward foreign currency commitments, meet the required definitions under reporting guidelines published by the IMF. All instruments are settled in foreign currency. Financial derivatives coverage: The RBA uses bond futures contracts, interest rate futures contracts, cross-currency basis swaps and foreign exchange swaps as part of the management of its foreign reserves portfolio. Foreign exchange swaps are also used to assist in domestic liquidity management; as these swaps involve the Australian dollar, they affect the level of gross reserves, but do not alter foreign currency liquidity. 2.4.1 Valuation Valuation: The values for SDRs and reserve position in the IMF are determined at the IMF basket valuation for the SDR. Gold is valued at the 3 p.m. price in the London gold market on the last business day of the month. The foreign currency value of other overseas assets is based, where applicable, on market quotations; accrued interest is normally taken into account. Derivative instruments are also revalued using market quotations. Investments in the Asian bond funds are revalued using pricing provided by the BIS. Conversion rates: The foreign currency value of overseas assets is converted at end-month market exchange rates. Aggregate data are disseminated in Australian dollars and U.S. dollars. Frequency of calculation and revaluation: Reserve assets and derivative instruments are revalued daily. 3.1.1 Source data collection programs Data sources: Data are largely obtained from the RBA's balance sheet, with the exception of data on the reserve position in the IMF and a share of Australia's holdings of SDRs that are not owned by the RBA. The latter are obtained from information made available to the RBA in its capacity as agent for the Australian Government in IMF transactions.
